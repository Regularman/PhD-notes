Uses electrode immersed electric boilers, where electric currents are ran through the water, converting into heat to turn water into steam. The water is circulated with a pump at the top surface where the electrodes are submersed.
- The electrodes have ceramic insulators to prevent steam leakage
- By increasing the submersion level of the electrode, steam production can be flexibly managed, this can be controlled through a PLC controller that controls the water circulation in the steam chamber
- The waste water is de-aerated, mixed with make-up water and returned as feed-water for the next cycle. Conductivity is controlled through chemical dosing and blowdown. Note that conductivity of the water is key

Alunorte also used fuel switching and biomass integration to make an impact to their carbon emissions.
- Switching from coal to natural gas from 2/3 of coal and fuel oil. This slashed carbon emissions by 700,000 tpa. Sulphates dropped by 70%, nitrates by 40%, and particulate matter by 30%. This costed $200 million and was externally financed
- Uses 140,000 t of acai kernels per year, displacing a fourth of the remaining third of coal and fuel oil consumption

Currently, Al is not written into the CABM. Many of AluNorte's investments are made based on future carbon tax price predictions.

- Partnership with Calix to try electric calcination by 2030, with full deployment by 2040.

## Notes

Currently, the alumina refinery uses 12 boilers to produce steam (6 heavy fuel oil, 3 electric, and 3 coal fired boilers). The electric boilers are used to take advantage of low price events during rainy seasons. Note that there was a previous project where the other two jet type electric boilers were retrofitted. However, it does not operate at the required performance and is utilised at 70% capacity and suffers frequent shutdowns. 

Note that the plant has a annual capacity of 6.3 Mtpa of alumina and also plans the implementation of electric calcination pilot by 2030. Full deployment is expected by 2040. Hydrogen calcination was evaluated and deemed less economical due to energy losses and high capex. Full electrification of the site will require 1.5GW of capacity, and the site was only designed for 110MW.
