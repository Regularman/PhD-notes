## Contributions

Compares $8$ different cost metrics for 2 base scenarios and 4 development scenarios to show how the choice of cost metrics can change the narrative of the decarbonisation of the power sector.

## Content

#### Net Present Cost

The net present cost is the discounted value of revenue minus the capital expenditure required to build the network and the operating expenditure of asset operation.

#### Bulk power electricity price

It represents the wholesale electricity price, or the average of cost of serving all of the electricity demand. It means that the cost can be recovered if everyone using the electricity pays $\$40/MWh$. 

This can be expressed as the marginal price. The marginal price is the cost to increase electricity generation. The marginal unit can be calculated using the shadow price of changing the constraints (as below)

- Load balancing constraint,
- Operating reserves constraint,
- Renewable or clean energy policy constraint, and
- Reserve margin constraint

The cumulative shadow cost of increasing each constraint divided by electricity demand to give the marginal cost of production of electricity.

#### Carbon abatement cost

This is the discounted system cost of a scenario from the reference case divided by the discounted difference in $CO_2$ emissions between the two scenarios

The marginal pricing is the shadow price from the $CO_2$ cap constraint that describes the cost of abating the next unit of $CO_2$ emissions.

#### A note on marginal versus average bulk energy price

The marginal price of production will always be higher than the average bulk energy price. For example, looking at the bulk and marginal prices, their price variation is influenced by different mechanisms.
- The marginal price flattens out after $2035$ when the power grid has been decarbonised, at which point, the cost of production for the marginal unit of electricity is much lower.
- Comparatively, the bulk electricity pricing continues to grow until late into the $2040s$. This is driven by a growth in the capital expenditure as more high cost units are being added to maintain the system's zero carbon status. After 2040, resources from the initial ramp up in the 2020s begins to be paid off, resulting in declining average price. Fuel and O&M costs also remains fairly constant after $2035$.
- Further, the marginal price of electricity is driven by limited transmission. New transmission beyond projects already is not allowed until $2026$, so the buildout of new low carbon technology pushes up the marginal cost of production until new transmission is able to relieve congestion and lower the cost of serving the next unit.

#### A note on using total system cost and retail rate

Even within cost considerations, the choice of metrics reflect the needs of different stakeholders. Where the use of the the system cost reflects the total present value of a system, the retail rate outlines how this is passed onto the consumer and depends on
- The distribution of costs between industrial and residential consumers. Historically, residential consumers pay $80\%$ more than industrial consumers
- The revenue model of the utility company

#### A note on electricity versus non-electricity expenditure 
The retail rate can then be split into the electricity and non-electricity expenditure. ![[Screenshot 2026-11-19 at 8.07.34 pm.png|371]]

As we can see, the constrained scenario has the highest absolute and normalised system cost of electricity. This is due to the inability to access cheap renewable electricity. 

For example, in the case of No CCS, total system cost is the highest, but when this is normalised against the electricity demand, then it becomes lower than other decarbonisation cases.

- The reference case still have the lowest normalised and absolute system cost. This reflects the fact that more infrastructure will be needed to supply electricity demand. For consumers, this gets reflected and recovered through increasing electricity bill, but is offset by reduction in non-electricity consumption.
