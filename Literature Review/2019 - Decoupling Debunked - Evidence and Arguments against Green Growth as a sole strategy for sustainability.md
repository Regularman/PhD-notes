Partial decoupling is where some impacts are externalised (burden shifting).

Permanence matters. It is not sufficient to achieve decoupling between GDP and environmental pressure. This decoupling must be durable in the face of increasing environmental pressure.
- Further this decoupling has to be within the planetary boundaries (3% GDP growth and -0.5% degrowth in emissions versus 3% growth in GDP vs 5% degrowth in emissions)
- Imacts also have to global and sufficiently fast

## Evidence against decoupling

What all of these studies look for is the Emission Kuznet's curve, the idea that economic growth will naturally lead to degrowth (natural evolution of the economy to a post-growth society)
- ~={red}In light of the present review, we can safely conclude that there is no empirical evidence supporting the existence of a decoupling of the type described as necessary in the first section of this report – that is an absolute, global, permanent, and sufficiently fast and large decoupling of environmental pressures (both resources and impacts) from economic growth.=~

There are literature which shows that material decoupling is only a result of burden shifting to another part of the economy. 
- Wiedmann et al. (2015) who show that using material footprint instead of Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) cancels an only apparent relative decoupling in the US, UK, Japan, the OECD, and EU-27. This is due to the effects of offshoring.

Further, evidence shows that recoupling is possible
- Szlavik and Szép, (2017) show that if absolute decoupling occurred at all, it lasted only during short periods and only in specific places, for example in Poland from 2011 to 2014. This ephemeral breaks in the coupling relation are most often explained by economic crises and political restructuring, and not by the continuous introduction of ever more efficient technologies and practices.

There are also examples of insufficient decoupling.
- In parts of Australia and California where absolute decoupling can be observed, water consumption remains at unsustainable levels, as evidenced by an increasing number of “anthropogenic droughts” (AghaKouchak et al., 2015; Ashraf et al., 2017). These should be seen as cases of insufficient decoupling.
- Countries which has decoupled water use with economic growth normally just externalise this impact to other countries through the import of virtual water.

Indeed studies which demonstrates absolute decoupling often shows infinitesimal degrowth patterns, or are limited in temporal or geographical boundaries. [Pg 25 of report]
- The risk of recoupling can be attributed to easy decarbonisation efforts such as the switch to gas.
- Most of these studies are also production based.

Land use also tells the story of the Global North externalising impacts to the Global South or developing countries. 
- In total 60% of land is used for exports (Weinzettel et al., 2013) whereas high-income countries are the greatest net importers. This mainly comes from demand for meat and agricultural products.

## Can decoupling potentially occur

Unlikely to happen due to 
- Rising energy expenditure
- Rebound effects
- Burden shifting
- Underestimated impact of services
- Limited potential of recycling
- Insufficient or inappropriate technological changes
- Cost shifting

#### Rising energy expenditure

The study talks about the increasing marginal cost of energy as more immediate resources are needed to extract resources once widely available stores have been depleted. The metric used to describe this is the EROI (Energy returned on energy invested). The lower the EROI, the higher the energy expenditure.
- There are different versions of the EROI. For example, the Point of Use EOI includes the costs of refining and transporting the fuel. Extended EROI is the energy required not only to get, but to use a unit of energy

There are studies which shows that economic growth is strictly limited by the EROI of the energy sources used by a country. Therefore, declining EROI in recent years highlights that a decoupled growth of economy and resource consumption is unlikely.
- Capellán-Pérez et al. (2018) simulate what would happen to average EROI by 2050 should renewable energy sources increase from 15% to 30% (1st scenario) and from 15% to 50% (2nd scenario). In the first scenario, average EROI drops from currently 6:1 to 5:1; and down to 3:1 in the second scenario. If energy expenditures play an important role in the dynamics of economic growth, this means that renewable energies are fundamentally unable to propel an economy as fast as fossil fuels.
- Indeed, the mineral intensity of renewable energies is higher than the one for fossil fuels – 1kWh of renewable energy requires 10 times more metals than 1kWh of fossil energy (Arnsperger and Bourg, 2017, p. 87).

#### Rebound effects

First order: Direct rebound effect is where efficiency gain is reinvested as additional consumption of the same product or service

Second Order: Indirect rebound effect describes the re-allocation of resources due to efficiency gain in a system/

Third Order: Efficiency in resource use can also rebound at the macro-level (economy-wide or macroeconomic rebound effect). For instance, efficiency gains in internal combustion engines have helped made private car transportation effective and affordable and resulted in a wide diffusion of this technology

Further, there are also negative rebound: 
- The authors (ibid. 7-77) also add a third category: a “negative rebound” for situations where actual energy savings are higher than expected (e.g. “a family that installs a new energy-efficient water heater may be motivated to find other ways to save energy by taking shorter showers, washing clothes in cold water, or by limiting dishwasher use to full loads”; negative rebound, better example, direct causality: isolating walls reduces heating demand, making existing heating installations oversized. This, in turn, requires installing new and smaller boilers, which are more efficient, so energy demand sinks again.
The rebound effect can also be classified as a partial or absolute rebound

Pg 38 provides a summary of studies that looks at the rebound effect

#### Problem shifting

The literature gave examples of how increase in renewable energy, nuclear energy, and natural gas burden shifts from emissions to biodiversity loss, pressure on critical minerals, and water use.

#### Servitisation does not equal dematerialisation

The so-called “service economy” carries a heavier biophysical backpack than one would think. In the countries with the most urgent mitigation imperatives, the service sector has already been developed to its maximum without the benefits of absolutely decreasing environmental pressures. Services have a footprint, that even though lower than manufactured products, is often only added on top of the environmental pressure pile without many substitutions occurring

- This means that servitisation is a layer on top of the existing intensive industrialized society rather than a substitution

#### Limits to recycling

- Materials have to be downcycled and new materials have to be introduced
- Further, a growing economy will have a growing input stream requirement, which recycling must also grow to match.
- Recycling rates is far form 100% (especially with miniaturisation and specialisation of products).
- . If the ability to recycle is slower than the will to produce, then virgin resources will have to be used due to the phenomenon of planned obsolence.
- There is also not enough waste to recycle in a growing economy. This would be like a snake trying to make a larger skin out of the scraps of its previous, smaller skin.

Arnsperger and Bourg (2017, p. 73) assume that the residence time of copper in the economy is of 40 years and that 60% of it can be recycled with current technologies. Out of the 6 million tons of copper used in 1975, this means that 4 million could have been recovered by 2015. However, consumption of copper has grown to 16 million in the last 40 years and so, despite recycling, 12 million tons of virgin copper must still be extracted. In this case, even with assuming an illusory 100% recycling rate, the extraction would have more than doubled during the period.

#### Issues with technology solutions

Not disruptive enough
As new energy sources are discovered, developed, and deployed, the old sources do not decline, instead, total energy use grows with additional layers on the energy mix cake. York (2012) finds that each unit of energy use from non-fossil fuel sources displaced less than one-quarter of a unit of its fossil-fuel counterpart, showing empirical support for the claim that expanding renewable energies is far from enough to curb fossil fuel consumption

Most evident if the need for demand reduction is the increase in renewable energy by 2.3 to 4.6 times, but also a parallel decrease in energy intensity of 2.8% per year.
- However currently, consumption outstrips substitution of green technology with old infrastructure
- That is, the pace of decarbonization technologies deployment is not high enough
- There are also decreasing marginal returns to innovation as questions are becoming increasingly narrow and intractable.

#### Cost shifting (Not of economic but environmental cost shifting)

Another reason for the externalising environmental impacts from economic developed to low income countries is that the latter often has more lax environmental regulations.

If that is so, it means that ecological sustainability can only be achieved via a downscaling of polluting production.
- Current modes of decoupling are just statistical tricks.
